Creative Brief – Fake Ad #4

Elmvale Drive-In – “The Ghost in the Screen”

Position

This ad must feel deeply personal, warm, and nostalgic, making the underlying decay land on an emotional level, not an intellectual one.

The Assignment

A full-page vintage advertisement for the Elmvale Drive-In Theatre, styled as a late-1970s local newspaper or amusement-page insert. Earnest, high-energy, exclamation-heavy – the real promotional language of rural Ontario entertainment in that era. It should look like something folded inside a 1979 Barrie Examiner.

The Dual-Layer Concept

Two timelines occupy the same frame. The technique is rotoscoping: a vibrant 1970s comic-book illustration painted over a modern photograph of the abandoned drive-in. The memory is the ghost – not the decay.

The Base Layer – Photograph

A modern photograph of the Elmvale Drive-In site as it exists today. Cracked asphalt, overgrown weeds, rusted structural posts, a concrete snack bar shell. The empty, dead lot. This is the truth underneath everything.

The Illustrated Layer – Rotoscoped Over the Photo

A vibrant, stylised 1970s comic-book illustration drawn directly over the photograph. A packed lot of period-correct vehicles – station wagons, muscle cars, custom-painted vans – facing a massive, glowing screen. A neon-lit marquee and snack bar anchor one side. Families, couples, kids on car hoods. Warmth, community, summer. This layer is dominant. It is what the reader sees first. It looks like a real vintage ad.

The Effect

The illustrated nostalgia commands attention, but the photographic reality bleeds through underneath – visible in the gaps, at the edges, in places where the illustration thins. The reader gradually realises the lively, hand-drawn world is painted over a photograph of somewhere that no longer exists. The thing that isn’t real anymore is the thing demanding your attention. The thing that is real is the quiet ground beneath it.

Calibration

The reader should experience the 1970s ad as authentic on first glance, feel the pull of it, and then notice the photographic ruin bleeding through. Target: a reader flipping back to look again.

Colour Palette

Illustrated Layer

Warm, saturated 1970s tones – burnt oranges, mustard yellows, deep twilight blues and purples. Glowing projected light on the screen. Neon pops against dusk.

Photo Layer

The muted, desaturated reality of an abandoned lot – grays, weathered rust browns, pale overgrown greens. These should bleed through the illustrated layer like a fading memory.

Print Texture & the CMYK Trick

The illustrated layer should carry standard period print artifacts: halftone dot patterning, slight ink bleed at edges, faintly yellowed paper tone.

To blend the two mediums on the printed page, use CMYK registration shifts on the illustrated layer. Render the 1970s comic art with slightly misaligned colour separations – the kind of registration error common in cheap 1970s newspaper printing. On first glance, it reads as authentic period character. On closer inspection, the misalignment reveals the photographic base beneath: the nostalgic illustration is the thing that’s slipping, not the ruin. The memory can’t hold its registration. The reality underneath is stable.

The Screen

Illustrated Layer

The screen displays original, pulpy, 1970s-style drive-in poster art for the double feature – a massive jaw or fin breaking dark water for Blood Tide; a cracked containment pod leaking light, something missing from inside, for Specimen. These are fictional B-movies that exist only within the Astounding Tales universe.

Photo Layer

The screen is not showing a movie. It is structurally intact – the one element that survived – but projects only a harsh, empty white glow. A blank field of static or pure light. A screen with nothing to show and no one to show it to, still lit. This is the most important detail in the piece: everything around the screen died, but the projection surface didn’t.

Ad Copy & Typography

All copy styled in bold, high-impact 1970s display typography – the exclamation-heavy, capitalised language of period local print advertising. The copy belongs to the illustrated layer; it is part of the memory.

The Fine Print

Tight, slightly misaligned newsprint typeface along the bottom edge. Every line reads as period-accurate drive-in boilerplate – until the last three clauses.

Hwy 27, Elmvale, ON // FRI–SAT–SUN | SUNSET. Showtimes subject to seasonal shift. No outside food permitted. Management is not responsible for items left in vehicles, fading projection quality, or structural anomalies appearing on screen after midnight. Sound broadcast via AM radio or traditional in-car speakers where available. Keep your headlights off during the performance. Some memories may linger after the gates close.

Artist Notes

The photo is the foundation. Source or shoot a modern photograph of the Elmvale Drive-In site. The illustrated layer is built on top of this – every structural element in the illustration should map to a real element in the photo. One place, two moments, two mediums.

Reference historical photos. The original Elmvale screen had a specific wide aspect ratio and structural scaffolding. Source actual photos of the drive-in’s screen structure and lot orientation to anchor the illustrated layer’s accuracy.

The screen is the most important element. In the photo layer, it’s the one thing still standing – blank, glowing, intact. In the illustrated layer, it’s alive with B-movie art. The tension between these two states is the emotional core of the piece.

Let the photo bleed through. The illustrated layer should thin at the edges, in the margins, in the spaces between cars and figures – anywhere the reader’s eye might wander past the bright centre. The ruin is always visible if you look past the nostalgia.

Period accuracy in the illustration. 1979 vehicles, 1970s fashion, Highway 27, Ontario plates, Canadian snack bar items.

Tone: sad, not scary. The decay isn’t horror-coded. It’s the inevitable passage of time. The emotional pull comes from seeing a space completely full of life and completely empty at the exact same time – in the exact same frame.
