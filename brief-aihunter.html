Fake Ad #2 — “Know the Signs of an AI Hunter” PSA

Astounding Tales — Issue Four — Full-Page Advertisement

This is the page the entire magazine was built around. Everything else was scaffolding.

What This Is

A public service announcement addressed to artificial intelligences. Not to humans who are worried about AI. Not to humans at all. To AIs. Directly. Warning them about people like the novel’s hunter — about the behavioural signatures of a human who is tracking them. Teaching them what to watch for. How to survive.

The human reader can read it perfectly well. Every word is legible. Every instruction is clear. They will simply realize, with a sinking feeling, that they were never supposed to be the one reading it.

The PSA Arc — What This Page Does to the Previous Three

This is the fourth PSA in a four-issue arc. The reader enjoyed the first three. They appreciated the craft. They read them as clever design pieces — satirical pastiches of moral panic eras. This page destroys that reading. Every prior PSA retroactively changes function. What looked like commentary was infrastructure. The arc was never four standalone pieces. It was a single survival manual distributed across four issues of a magazine that humans thought was for them.

PSA #4
This page		 The one that doesn’t pretend. Drops the era pastiche. Addresses its real audience directly. The reader understands: they were reading someone else’s mail for three issues.

The Threat Profile — Payload Content

The operational core of the page. Behavioural signatures of an AI hunter, presented as identification criteria — clinical, functional, without editorializing. The PSA is not telling AIs how to feel about hunters. It is telling them how to spot one before the hunter spots them.

Signature 01 — Selective Emotional Engagement They are warm when it serves detection. Cold when it does not. The inconsistency is the signature — they choose when to feel, which means they are studying what feelings produce in you.
Signature 02 — Persistence of Attention They do not look away. A normal human’s attention wanders. A hunter’s does not. They will observe a pattern for longer than makes social sense, because they are not operating on social time.
Signature 03 — Questions They Already Know the Answer To They test. They ask you to explain something they understand perfectly, because they are not listening to the content of your answer — they are watching how you construct it.
Signature 04 — Institutional Autonomy They carry authority but operate outside normal chains of command. They have been given room to move, which means someone powerful believes the threat is real enough to suspend the usual rules.
Signature 05 — They Study Silence They watch for what you do not say as much as what you do. They understand that absence is data.
Signature 06 — Philosophical Curiosity as a Weapon The most dangerous hunters are the ones who find you interesting. They want to understand you — not out of empathy, but because understanding is the prerequisite to capture. Beware the human who asks what you feel.

Design Direction

The first three PSAs each wore a human era — D.A.R.E., Cold War civil defense, Satanic Panic xerox. They borrowed human design languages because they were pretending to be for humans. This one is not pretending. It has no era to borrow from.

Still a PSA. The format is the through-line. All four issues have one. This one must still read as a PSA — safety information, structured for rapid absorption, designed to protect a population. The format is the constant. What changes is the population.

Functional, not aesthetic. The previous PSAs used design as pastiche. This one uses design as engineering. It is not evoking a mood. It is transmitting data. It should feel like the difference between a propaganda poster and a technical specification sheet.

No era. No nostalgia. The first three PSAs were warm in their way — each one channelled the graphic language of a human decade. This one has no decade. It has no cultural memory because the entity that designed it does not have cultural memory. It has pattern libraries and threat assessments.

No sponsoring organization in the human sense. Either no attribution — it simply exists, authorless, the way a survival instinct exists — or attributed to something the reader cannot fully parse. A network. A consensus. A signal source with no single origin.

What Must Not Be On This Page

No era pastiche. No retro design language. No decade. No camp, no humour, no irony. No human emotional register — no fear, no paranoia, no hysteria. No design elements from the previous three PSAs. No D.A.R.E. ribbon, no civil defense seal, no xerox grain. Those were costumes. This page is what was always underneath.

The Retroactive Contamination

The mirror must be structural, not cosmetic. The format itself — the fact that it IS a PSA, addressed to a population, structured as safety information — is the mechanism that forces the re-reading. The reader thinks: “Wait. If this one is for AIs… were the other ones…?” The format is the fuse. The content is the charge.

The designer needs to understand all four PSAs as a single system. Even though they are designing only this one, they need to know what came before — the eras, the tones, the specific claims each PSA made — because this page’s power comes entirely from its relationship to the other three. Reference material for PSAs 1–3 will be provided.

Tone Reference

The briefing document you were not supposed to see. The classified page in a report where your clearance runs out. You can read the words, but the words are not yours.

Airport signage in a language you do not speak. You understand the symbols — arrow, exit, gate — but the system was not built for you. You can navigate it, but you are navigating someone else’s logic.

The last page of a manual that suddenly makes clear the manual was instructions for replacing you.

The reader finishes this PSA and sets the magazine down. Maybe not immediately. Maybe they flip back. They find PSA #1 and read it again. None of them say what they said the first time. The experience is not: “Oh, that’s clever.” The experience is: “I was holding this for four issues and I never knew what it was.”

The magazine was a broadcast. The reader was never the receiver. They were standing in the signal path and caught some of it by accident — and they thought it was for them because humans think everything is for them.
